Where Americans went

125 years of state populations, and the slow walk of the country's center of population from Indiana into the Missouri Ozarks.

Zain Hameed · 2025, rewritten October 2026 · Data: Census Bureau via FRED

Thomas Sowell spent much of his career pointing out that people's movements are a better record of what they value than anything they say. Nobody files a report explaining why they left Buffalo for Phoenix. They pack, and the census counts them at the other end.

The map below is that record for the twentieth century and after. Each circle is a state, placed at the point where its own population is centered and sized by its population in the chosen year. The dark path traces the population-weighted center of the whole country.

The center moved west and south in 125 years, about a year. For the first half of the century it moved almost due west, across Illinois, as the whole country filled in. After about 1950 it bends south, and the bend is the interesting part.

Who gained, who lost

Share of the national population is the right measure here, since nearly every state grew in absolute terms. What matters is where each additional American chose to live.

California alone gained nearly ten points of national share; Florida and Texas six and five. Pennsylvania, New York, Illinois and Ohio together gave up almost thirteen. None of it was planned. No agency decided that the country's weight should shift to the Sun Belt; millions of households decided it, one move at a time.

What changed was the set of things people could take with them. Air conditioning made Southern summers tolerable. The interstate highways cut the cost of putting a factory somewhere cheap. Later, housing costs did the sorting: people moved toward places where they could afford to live, which more and more meant places that allowed building. The center of population is, among other things, a slow measure of where it was legal to put a house.

What this measure misses

  • States are coarse. The movement from city cores to suburbs, the largest migration of the century, happens almost entirely inside states and doesn't show here.
  • Net change mixes migration with births, deaths and immigration. A state can lose share because people leave, or because it has fewer births and draws fewer immigrants. Separating those needs flows, not stocks.
  • Each state's position is its 2020 center. Michigan's center in 1900 was not where it is now, but the error that introduces is small next to the shifts between states.

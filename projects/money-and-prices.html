Money and prices

Sixty years of money growth and inflation in every country with data. Where Friedman's rule holds, and where it fades.

Zain Hameed · October 2026 · Data: World Bank

Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon.Milton Friedman, 1963

Friedman's sentence is often quoted as a slogan, but it is also a prediction you can check. If a country's money supply grows faster than its output, the difference should show up as rising prices, roughly one for one, over periods long enough for the short-run noise to wash out. That is a bold claim in Popper's sense. It names a number, and the world could easily have refused to match it.

The test below uses every country the World Bank reports, averaged by decade: country-decades from the 1960s to the 2020s. On the horizontal axis is how fast broad money grew minus how fast real output grew. On the vertical axis is inflation. If Friedman is right, the points should sit on the diagonal.

Over the whole sample, the fit is remarkable: a slope of and a correlation of . But that summary is carried by the high-inflation cases, and the interesting part is what happens when you split them up.

Above 40% a year, money growth and inflation move together almost exactly. Few relationships in macroeconomics fit this well. Every very high inflation in the data, from Bolivia and Peru in the 1980s to Brazil and the post-Soviet economies in the 1990s, comes with money growth of about the same size.

Below 10%, the relationship nearly disappears. That is not evidence against the quantity theory so much as evidence about what it leaves out. When money grows slowly, the noise in how much money people want to hold (velocity, in the jargon) is as large as the signal. Financial innovation changes what counts as money. And central banks that target inflation adjust the money supply to hit their target, so money becomes a response to inflation as much as a cause. The rule is a law about large numbers that gets drowned out in small ones.

That is a more useful conclusion than either "Friedman was right" or "monetarism failed." If you are trying to explain 200% inflation, start with the central bank's balance sheet. If you are trying to explain why inflation was 3% rather than 2%, money growth alone won't tell you.

Method

World Bank indicators FM.LBL.BMNY.ZG (broad money growth), FP.CPI.TOTL.ZG (consumer price inflation) and NY.GDP.MKTP.KD.ZG (real GDP growth), 1960 onward, every economy that isn't an aggregate. Rates are averaged within each decade in logs, so that a year of 1,000% followed by a year of −50% doesn't average to 475%, and a country-decade needs at least five years with all three series. Slopes are least-squares fits of log inflation on log excess money growth within each band. Broad money definitions differ across countries and change over time, which adds noise at the low end.

The great stagnation, measured

American productivity grew 2.8% a year for a quarter century after the war, then about half that. What the gap adds up to.

Zain Hameed · October 2026 · Data: Bureau of Labor Statistics and BEA via FRED, Penn World Table

In 2011 Tyler Cowen argued that the United States had spent the twentieth century harvesting low-hanging fruit: free land, a backlog of transformative inventions, and a population that could be sent to school for the first time. By the 1970s most of that fruit had been eaten, and growth slowed. The argument was controversial, but its central number is not. Here it is.

On a log scale, a constant growth rate is a straight line, and the chart is two slopes with a detour. From 1948 to 1973 output per hour grew a year. From 1973 to 1995 it grew . A burst between 1995 and 2005, when computers and the internet finally showed up in the productivity statistics, ran at , and then the slow pace returned.

Had the postwar pace simply continued, American workers would now produce more per hour than they do. That is not a forecast or a model. It is the arithmetic of a rate that held for twenty-five years and then stopped.

Total factor productivity tells the same story more starkly. It is the closest thing economics has to a measure of new ideas put to work, and in both slow eras it grew at about a quarter of a percent a year. Some of the slowdown may be mismeasurement, since a free search engine adds little to GDP. But the slowdown started in the 1970s, decades before the goods that are hardest to measure arrived.

Since 2019 productivity has grown a year. It is tempting to call that the start of a new burst. Six years is too short to say, and productivity figures get revised. The honest position is to write down now what would count as evidence, so it can't be redefined later: say, another five years above 2%.

Why the decimal point matters

Cowen's later book, Stubborn Attachments, makes the moral case: because growth compounds, small differences in the rate become large differences in how people live, and they keep growing. A growth rate is a fact about the future that most people never think about.

The gap between 1% and 2% growth, the gap between the stagnant eras and the good ones, is the gap between a child born today being about twice as rich at seventy-five as the average American is now, or more than four times as rich. Few economic questions have stakes that large, and few get less attention.
